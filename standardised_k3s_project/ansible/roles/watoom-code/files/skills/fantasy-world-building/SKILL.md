# Fantasy World-Building Guidelines

## Quick reference

| Element | Guiding principle | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Magic | Clear rules | Limitations matter more than powers |
| Setting | Internal consistency | Every rule has a reason |
| Peoples and creatures | Distinctive and logical | Avoid simple human variants |
| History | At least three generations | The past affects the present |
| Politics | Clear distribution of power | Conflicts have underlying causes |

## Core principles: Sanderson's laws of magic

1. **Readers' satisfaction with magic resolving a problem is proportional to their understanding of it.** Establish rules before magic solves conflicts. Soft, mysterious magic creates atmosphere and wonder; explicit hard magic can resolve problems.
2. **Limitations are more interesting than powers.** Strong magic needs significant costs. Constraints create conflict and strategy; flawless systems offer little drama.
3. **Expand existing elements before adding new ones.** Deepen established ideas. Connected systems are stronger than isolated additions; let complexity grow organically.

## Designing magic systems

### Hard magic: explicit rules
Define:
1. **Energy source:** Where does magic come from? Is the supply finite or unlimited? Can it be exhausted?
2. **Rules of use:** Who can use it, how is it acquired, and what conditions and restrictions apply?
3. **Costs and consequences:** What does use cost? What happens with overuse? Are there lasting effects?
4. **Possibilities and impossibilities:** What can magic do, what explicitly cannot be done, and where are its boundaries?

Example:
~~~text
System: Elemental manipulation.
Source: Each person is born with one elemental affinity.
Rules: Users manipulate only their own element, which must be present nearby.
Limitations: Mental fatigue; overuse causes elemental backlash.
Cost: Magic consumes vitality, recovered through rest.
Prohibitions: Elements cannot be created, only manipulated. Elements inside living bodies cannot be controlled.
~~~

### Soft magic: mystery
Readers do not fully know the rules. Use it for atmosphere, wonder and awe, rather than conveniently resolving the central conflict.
Suitable uses include ancient, partly forgotten magic; divine powers beyond mortal understanding; background wonders and world-building; and supporting incidents that do not determine the main resolution.

## World-building framework

### Geography and environment
1. **Terrain:** How do mountains, rivers and seas affect civilizations? How does climate shape culture? How does resource distribution affect the economy?
2. **Ecosystems:** What are the food chains? How do magical creatures fit their environments? How do humans and other intelligent peoples survive?
3. **Magic's geographical effects:** How does magic change nature? Has it created landmarks? What traces remain from magical disasters?

### Society and culture
- **Politics:** Government forms such as monarchy, democracy or theocracy; succession and transfer of power; magic's political effects; factional conflicts.
- **Economics:** What is traded and why; currency; magic's economic effects; resource scarcity.
- **Society:** Class structures, relations between peoples, the status of magic users, education and knowledge transmission.
- **Culture:** Religion and belief, art and entertainment, festivals and traditions, values and taboos.

### Historical depth
Develop at least three generations, with older history where relevant:
- **Present:** Current conditions, major conflicts and problems, leading powers and figures.
- **Parents' generation,30–50 years earlier:** Events that shaped the present, older people's memories, unresolved legacies.
- **Grandparents' generation,60–100 years earlier:** Legends and stories, lost knowledge, historical wounds.
- **Ancient past:** Myths, civilizations' rise and fall, and origins explaining the present setting.

## Designing intelligent peoples and creatures

### Intelligent peoples
Avoid a single defining trait: not all elves are elegant and noble, all dwarves greedy and ill-tempered, or all orcs savage and warlike.
Create depth through internal cultural and moral diversity, individual personalities, and complex histories containing good and bad.
Give traits reasons: How do physical features help survival? Where do cultural practices come from? What history shapes relations with other peoples?

### Magical creatures
Define:
1. **Ecological niche:** Role, food, predators and reproduction.
2. **Source of magic:** Why they have it, how it aids survival, and its costs or limitations.
3. **Human relations:** Whether they are dangerous or useful, can be domesticated, and how people respond.

## Common pitfalls

### Over-explaining
Problem: Chapters of exposition halt the story.
Reveal the setting through action. Explain what characters need to know, let readers assemble some details, and make information serve plot or character.

### Inconsistent rules
Problem: World or magic rules change for plot convenience.
Establish major rules in advance and track them. Foreshadow exceptions. Let characters solve problems creatively within established limits.

### Defaulting to medieval Europe
Problem: Every fantasy setting becomes a copy of medieval Europe.
Explore other cultures and periods, combine cultural influences, create distinctive social structures, and consider how magic changes society's development.

### The chosen-one trap
Problem: Prophecy or ancestry makes the protagonist special instead of their actions.
Make choices matter. Even prophesied heroes must work toward their outcomes. Subvert or examine the trope; emphasize growth over innate gifts.

## Fantasy subgenres
- **High/epic fantasy:** Invented worlds, good-versus-evil struggles, epic scale and stakes, magic as part of the world.
- **Low fantasy:** Real-world settings with magical elements, smaller personal stakes, rare or mysterious magic, grounded tone.
- **Urban fantasy:** Modern cities, hidden magical worlds, frequent detective or mystery elements, collisions between the two worlds.
- **Dark fantasy:** Moral ambiguity, horror and violence, antiheroes, harsher consequences.

## Applying the upstream Novel-Writer workflow
The upstream names below describe writing stages, not installed WatooM Code slash commands. Use the existing native tools and Story Skills conventions.
- **Specification, upstream /specify:** Define magic, peoples and geography; identify the subgenre; list major rules to establish; plan the pace of revelation.
- **Planning, upstream /plan:** Connect world elements to plot, plan demonstrations of magic, design cultural encounters and clashes, maintain consistency.
- **Drafting, upstream /write:** Show the world through action rather than information dumps. Let characters react to it, use sensory detail, preserve established rules.
- **Analysis, upstream /analyze:** Check world-rule consistency and magic-system logic. Confirm setting elements have a purpose and serve the story.

## World-building checklist
- [ ] Magic has clear rules and limitations.
- [ ] Geography and climate affect culture and plot.
- [ ] History has at least three generations of depth.
- [ ] Multiple political powers have clear motivations.
- [ ] Economics and trade have a logical basis.
- [ ] Peoples and cultures have internal diversity.
- [ ] Magical creatures have ecological niches and purposes.
- [ ] Religion and belief affect the world.
- [ ] Social structures define classes and mobility.
- [ ] Rules remain consistent throughout the story.

## Revealing information

### The iceberg principle
Create the full underlying world, but reveal only10–20%. Readers need not know everything. Unseen depth supports confidence and consistency; the author knows more than appears on the page.

### Timing
- **First chapter:** Basic orientation rather than exhaustive explanation, one compelling magical/fantastical element, the protagonist's place in the world.
- **First quarter:** Core magic rules, major peoples/cultures, central conflict's roots in the setting.
- **Middle:** Deepen established elements, complicate politics, reveal historical influences.
- **Later sections:** Connect setting threads, expose deeper history, show how established world elements contribute to resolving conflict.

## Reader expectations
Fantasy readers seek immersive, believable worlds; consistent and interesting magic; complex cultures and politics; fresh treatments of familiar tropes; and worlds that feel larger than their stories.
Common frustrations include inconsistent rules, plot-convenient magic, thin medieval-European imitation, shallow settings, and information dumps rather than organic revelation.

World-building supports the story rather than becoming its purpose. The setting serves character and plot, while its rules shape and constrain characters' choices. Balance depth with narrative flow.
